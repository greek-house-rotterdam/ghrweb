## From Asia Minor to the Netherlands

In the early 20th century, amidst the political turmoil and displacement following the collapse of the Ottoman Empire in 1922 and the uprooting of indigenous Greek populations from Asia Minor, some refugees found sanctuary in the Netherlands.

## Preparation and Founding

These displaced people shared the same needs as the first Greeks who had settled in the Netherlands: to come together, preserve their identity, and build a church. However, times were difficult, and the growing threat of war in Europe delayed this dream.

Their wish finally came true after the end of World War II. In July 1946, the Association of Greeks in the Netherlands was founded, based in Rotterdam—a city that was rapidly becoming the world's leading port.

## The First Steps

During the first board meeting, Professor Dr. Sophia Antoniadou from Leiden University proposed building a church, as the concept of a Greek community was inseparable from a Greek Orthodox church. It would also serve the spiritual needs of the many Greek and other Orthodox sailors passing through the port.

After years of fundraising efforts and thanks to a generous land donation from the Municipality of Rotterdam—located in what was then called Museum Boymans van Beuningen Park—construction began on the Holy Church of Saint Nicholas.

## The Construction of Saint Nicholas Church

Built in a Byzantine basilica style and dedicated to the patron saint of all sailors, the church was inaugurated on June 29, 1957. At that time, there were 41 Greeks registered in the Consulate's records.

## Creating the Greek School

Beyond serving religious needs, the Association became a hub for the education of Greek children. Starting in 1959, the first one-room school operated every weekend inside the church, keeping our traditions, identity, and culture alive.

## Expanding the Association

![The Association's building since 1961, at Van Vollenhovenstraat 18](/images/eeo-building-1961.jpg)

On September 20, 1961, the Association purchased the three-story building at Van Vollenhovenstraat 18, very close to the church. It housed the offices and served as the priest's residence. For several decades, the ground floor has functioned as a social club for members and parishioners.

Between 1965 and 1971, the social club was converted into a school, with the first teacher seconded from the Greek state following a request from the board.

## Social Contribution

A key goal of the Association was to foster good relations with other social groups. This was achieved through church bazaars, an annual ecumenical service on the Sunday of Orthodoxy, lectures, and musical evenings. The highlight of the year was the annual ball, attended by about 500 people from leading Rotterdam businesses.

The Association always operated with a small number of members, but this never dampened its spirit. For 53 years, it maintained the Holy Church of Saint Nicholas—the only Greek Orthodox church in the Netherlands until 1987—and supported the priest, providing him with a home.

As fundraising events became less successful due to the economic crisis and donations dwindled, the Association could no longer cover the church's maintenance costs. Thus, on November 26, 2010, the church was officially donated to the Holy Metropolis of Belgium and Exarchate of the Netherlands and Luxembourg. While the Association owned the property, the church had always been under the religious jurisdiction of the Metropolis.
