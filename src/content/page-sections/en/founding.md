## From Asia Minor to the Netherlands

In the early 20th century, amidst the political turmoil and ethnic cleansing that followed the collapse of the Ottoman Empire in 1922 and the displacement of the indigenous Greek populations of Asia Minor, some refugees found their way to the Netherlands.

## Preparations and Founding

These displaced people shared the same needs as the first Greeks who had settled in the Netherlands: to unite, to preserve their identity, and to build a church. However, times were difficult, and the growing threat of war in Europe delayed the realization of this dream.

Their wish finally came true after the end of World War II, in July 1946, when the Association of Greeks in the Netherlands was founded. The Association was based in Rotterdam, which was quickly evolving into the world's leading port.

## The First Steps

During the first board meeting, Professor Dr. Sophia Antoniadou from Leiden University proposed the construction of a church, as the concept of a Greek community was unthinkable without a Greek Orthodox place of worship. At the same time, it would serve the spiritual needs of the many Greek and other Orthodox sailors passing through the port.

After years of fundraising efforts and thanks to a generous land donation from the Municipality of Rotterdam — a plot in what was then called Museum Boymans van Beuningen Park — the construction of the Holy Church of St. Nicholas began.

## The Building of St. Nicholas Church

The church, built in a Byzantine basilica style and dedicated to the patron saint of all sailors, was inaugurated on June 29, 1957. That year, there were only 41 Greeks registered in the Consulate's records.

## The Creation of the Greek School

Beyond serving the religious needs of the community, the Association also became a hub for the education of Greek children. Starting in 1959, the first one-room school operated every weekend inside the church, preserving our traditions, identity, and culture.

## Expanding the Association

![The Association's building since 1961, at Van Vollenhovenstraat 18](/images/eeo-building-1961.jpg)

On September 20, 1961, the Association purchased the three-story building at Van Vollenhovenstraat 18, very close to the church. It housed the Association's offices and also served as the residence for the priest. For several decades now, the ground floor has functioned as a clubhouse for the Association's members and parishioners.

Between 1965 and 1971, the clubhouse was converted into a school, with the first teacher seconded from the Greek state following a request by the board of directors.

## Social Contribution

![The pillars of the canteen: Maria Kokkinou and Piet Haringsma](/images/eeo-canteen-founders.jpg)

A key goal of the Association was to promote good relations with other social groups. This was achieved through church bazaars, an annual ecumenical service on the Sunday of Orthodoxy, lectures, and music nights. A highlight of the year was the annual ball, which was attended by about 500 people from leading Rotterdam businesses.

The Association always operated with a small number of members, but this never dampened its dynamism. For 53 years, it maintained the Holy Church of St. Nicholas — the only Greek Orthodox church in the Netherlands until 1987 — as well as the priest, for whom it provided housing.

As fundraising events became less successful due to the economic crisis and donations ceased for the same reason, the Association could no longer cover the church's maintenance costs. Thus, on November 26, 2010, through a deed of donation, it was transferred to the Holy Metropolis of Belgium and Exarchate of the Netherlands and Luxembourg. While the Association owned the property, the church had always been under the religious jurisdiction of the Metropolis.

## The Association Today

Today, the Association's mission continues through the cultural group *The Greek House in Rotterdam*, founded in 2017, which organizes events, classes, and cultural activities for the Greek community in the Netherlands. With the partial renovation of the building in 2022 and the restoration of the facade in 2024, the Greek House remains the heart of the community.

In 2026, we celebrate 80 years since the founding of the Association — eight decades of Greek culture and community in the Netherlands.
