## Ioannis Prigos and "the world's largest commercial center"

The recorded history of the Greek community in the Netherlands dates back to around 1760.

The events of that era are documented in the famous *"Chronicles of Amsterdam"* written by Johannes Brink. Brink was born as Ioannis Prigos in the small mountain village of Zagora in Pelion, and later settled in Amsterdam — which he described in his Chronicle as "the world's largest commercial center."

## "But who are you?"

From the descriptions in his diary, we learn many details about the Greeks living in the Netherlands in the 18th century.

The shared dream of a small group of merchants in Amsterdam — in the prosperous heart of the Netherlands — was to create a place of worship that would serve their religious needs and act as a hub for fostering connections and preserving Greek identity, culture, and traditions. Above all, they wanted to protect their faith in the distant Lutheran country where they had settled.

They approached the city council around 1763, asking for permission to celebrate the Orthodox Divine Liturgy. "But who are you?" was the question that followed. "Greeks and Christians," they replied. Permission was granted and, according to the Chronicles, two Greek businessmen — Pigazis and Avgerinos — offered the attic of their commercial shops to be used as a place of worship.

## July 22, 1764: The first Divine Liturgy

The first Divine Liturgy took place on July 22, 1764, led by the Reverend Anthimos of Zagora. Soon, the small loft was filled — mostly by passing Greek and Russian Orthodox sailors.

Among the congregation, one would often find intellectuals, Dutch Hellenists, and clergymen, drawn there by the contemplative atmosphere of the service and the opportunity to witness the Divine Liturgy in the original Greek language — rather than the "Erasmian Greek" they had been taught at the great Dutch universities.

## Acquiring the Greek Church

The need for a Greek church could no longer be postponed — and this need was met through a series of coincidences that reads like a thriller.

In 1748, in Batavia (the distant Dutch colony in the Indies), a very wealthy Greek merchant named Dimitris Papathanasis passed away. Before he died, he entrusted the Dutch governor with the fate of the 15,417 gold florins he had accumulated with his late partner, Konstantina Mavroudoglou. The fortune was to go to their heirs, who were living at the time in Philippopolis, Bulgaria.

The wish was carried out by the conscientious Governor, and the money was sent to the Netherlands and deposited in the *"Orphan Chamber"* (Weeskamer), an institution that managed the inheritances of widows and orphans. Thus, one day in 1762, two of the heirs arrived in Amsterdam to claim the fortune.

As a sign of gratitude for the help provided by the local Greek community — and especially by the priest Reverend Nektarios — in obtaining the necessary proof of identity, the heirs donated the significant sum of 5,000 florins for the purchase of a three-story building at Oudezijds Voorburgwal no. 91. This building was converted into a church.

Thus, on a Sunday morning in May 1764, the first divine liturgy took place in the new Greek-Russian Orthodox Church of Saint Catherine — named after the church's patroness, Catherine the Great, Empress of Russia. The building still stands in Amsterdam today. On the same street, between 1772–1778, lived another Greek merchant, [Adamantios Korais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adamantios_Korais) — who would later become one of the greatest visionaries in the struggle for Greece's liberation from the Ottoman Empire.

Despite a constant lack of funds, divine liturgies were held more or less regularly — depending on the availability of a priest.

## The Russian connection and the closure of 1867

During the 1830s, new life was breathed into the Orthodox community when the Russian Princess Anna Pavlovna came to the Netherlands to marry Prince William of Orange. She donated several items to the Orthodox churches of the royal palaces and prayed at the Church of Saint Catherine, of which she became a major benefactress. Rich gifts were also given to the church by the brother of Tsar Nicholas I.

Unfortunately, in 1867, after the death of Anna Pavlovna, the church was forced to close due to a lack of resources, and the building was sold at auction. The Tsar's gifts were transferred to the Russian consulate.
