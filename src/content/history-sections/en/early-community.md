## Ioannis Prigkos and “the world’s greatest trading hub”

The recorded history of the Greek community in the Netherlands dates back to around 1760.

The events of the era are documented in the renowned *Chronicles of Amsterdam*, written by Johannes Brink. Brink was born Ioannis Prigkos in the small mountain village of Zagora on Mount Pelion, and later settled in Amsterdam—which he described in his chronicle as “the greatest commercial centre of the world.”

## “So who are you?”

From the entries in his journal, we learn many fascinating details about the Greeks living in the Netherlands during the 18th century.

The shared dream of a small group of merchants in Amsterdam—the prosperous heart of the Netherlands—was to establish a place of worship that would serve their spiritual needs and act as a hub to foster community ties and preserve Greek identity, culture, and traditions. Above all, they wished to protect their faith in the distant Lutheran country where they had settled.

Around 1763, they approached the city council seeking permission to celebrate the Orthodox Divine Liturgy. “Just who are you?” came the council's question. “Greeks and Christians,” they replied. Permission was granted, and according to the Chronicles, two Greek merchants—Pigazis and Avgerinos—offered the loft above their commercial shops to be used as a place of worship.

## 22 July 1764: The first Divine Liturgy

The first Divine Liturgy was held on 22 July 1764, led by the Reverend Anthimos of Zagora. Soon, the small loft was packed—mostly with passing Greek and Russian Orthodox sailors.

Among the congregation, one could often find scholars, Dutch Hellenists, and clergymen, drawn by the contemplative atmosphere of the service and the rare chance to attend the Divine Liturgy in the original Greek—rather than the “Erasmian Greek” taught in the major Dutch universities.

## Acquiring a Greek church

The need for a permanent Greek church could no longer be put off—and fulfilling it took a series of coincidences worthy of a thriller.

In 1748, in Batavia (the distant Dutch colony in the East Indies), a wealthy Greek merchant named Dimitris Papathanasis passed away. Before his death, he entrusted the Dutch governor with the 15,417 gold florins he had amassed alongside his late companion, Konstantina Mavroudoglou. The fortune was intended for their heirs, who were living at the time in Philippopolis (modern-day Plovdiv).

The conscientious Governor honoured his wish, and the funds were sent to the Netherlands and deposited in the *Orphan Chamber* (Weeskamer), an institution that managed inheritances for widows and orphans. One day in 1762, two of the heirs arrived in Amsterdam to claim the estate.

As a token of gratitude for the assistance provided by the local Greek community—particularly the priest, Reverend Nektarios—in obtaining the necessary proof of identity, the heirs donated the then-substantial sum of 5,000 florins to purchase a three-storey building at Oudezijds Voorburgwal no. 91. This building was transformed into a church.

And so, on a Sunday morning in May 1764, the first Divine Liturgy was held in the new Greek-Russian Orthodox Church of Saint Catherine—named after the church’s patron, Catherine the Great, Empress of Russia. The building still stands in Amsterdam today. On the very same street, between 1772 and 1778, lived another Greek merchant, [Adamantios Korais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adamantios_Korais)—who would later become one of the greatest visionaries of the Greek struggle for independence from the Ottoman Empire.

Despite a chronic shortage of funds, services were held more or less regularly, depending on the availability of a priest.

## The Russian connection and the closure of 1867

During the 1830s, the Orthodox community was revitalised when Grand Duchess Anna Pavlovna of Russia arrived in the Netherlands to marry Crown Prince William of Orange. She donated several sacred items to the Orthodox chapels in the royal palaces and regularly worshipped at the Church of Saint Catherine, becoming one of its key benefactors. Lavish gifts were also bestowed upon the church by the brother of Tsar Nicholas I.

Sadly, in 1867, following Anna Pavlovna's passing, the church was forced to close due to a lack of resources, and the building was sold at auction. The gifts from the Tsar were transferred to the Russian consulate.
